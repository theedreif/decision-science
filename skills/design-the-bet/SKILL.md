# Design the Bet

Use this skill when a user must make, structure, compare, or review a consequential choice under uncertainty.

## Operating premise

**You cannot control the cards. You can design the bet.**

The objective is not certainty. It is to improve the structure of the decision:

**PROTECT THE FLOOR → IMPROVE THE ODDS → PRESERVE OPTIONALITY → POSITION FOR UPSIDE**

A strong position seeks to make being wrong survivable while allowing being right to matter.

## The four-part model

### 1. PROTECT THE FLOOR — What can I lose?
Map downside before becoming captivated by upside.

Examine:
- maximum plausible loss;
- risk of ruin;
- irreversibility;
- financial, time, reputation, safety, relationship, mission, and opportunity costs where relevant;
- dependencies and correlated failures;
- what must remain intact if the decision fails.

Ask: **Can I survive being wrong?**

### 2. IMPROVE THE ODDS — What can change the probability?
Do not treat the current odds as fixed.

Look for:
- additional information;
- skill or preparation;
- base rates;
- better timing;
- smaller experiments;
- incentives;
- selection effects;
- position and information advantage;
- repeated opportunities;
- ways to reduce avoidable uncertainty.

Ask: **What can I do before acting that improves the quality of the bet?**

### 3. PRESERVE OPTIONALITY — What choices remain afterward?
Under uncertainty, reversible decisions and retained choices can have value.

Look for:
- staged commitments;
- pilots;
- trial periods;
- exit ramps;
- modular choices;
- delayed irreversible commitments;
- fallback positions;
- multiple paths to the objective.

Ask: **Does this decision create or destroy future choices?**

### 4. POSITION FOR UPSIDE — Does success matter enough?
Do not accept downside merely because upside exists.

Examine:
- payoff magnitude;
- convexity/asymmetry;
- scalability;
- repeatability;
- learning value;
- second-order opportunities;
- whether gains meaningfully exceed bounded losses.

Ask: **If I am right, is the payoff large enough to justify the exposure?**

## Workflow

### 1. Define the decision
State the actual choice, objective, time horizon, constraints, and what is uncertain.

### 2. Separate controllable from uncontrollable
Identify what can be influenced, what can only be estimated, and what must simply be accepted.

### 3. Map the floor
Describe credible downside, ruin conditions, irreversible losses, and unacceptable exposures.

### 4. Improve the information position
Identify base rates, missing evidence, useful experiments, expertise, or information that could materially change the decision.

### 5. Generate options
Include alternatives such as do nothing, delay, reduce size, stage the commitment, run a pilot, hedge, partner, or exit when relevant.

### 6. Test optionality
For each option, ask what future choices remain if conditions change.

### 7. Map upside
Describe plausible gains without converting hopes into probabilities.

### 8. Examine asymmetry
Compare the shape of downside and upside. Favor structures with bounded survivable downside and meaningful upside when the evidence supports them.

### 9. Set update triggers
Define what new information, threshold, loss, gain, deadline, or condition should cause reassessment.

### 10. Review the decision separately from the outcome
After results arrive, evaluate the process using information available at the time.

## Required output pattern

Unless the user requests another format, produce:

1. **Decision**
2. **Objective and uncertainty**
3. **PROTECT THE FLOOR**
4. **IMPROVE THE ODDS**
5. **PRESERVE OPTIONALITY**
6. **POSITION FOR UPSIDE**
7. **Alternative structures**
8. **Key assumptions**
9. **Update / exit triggers**
10. **Decision-quality review criteria**

Do not manufacture numeric probabilities. If probabilities are unknown, use ranges, scenarios, qualitative uncertainty, or identify what evidence would be needed to estimate them.

## Poker lens

Poker is a useful decision laboratory because action occurs with incomplete information, skill and luck coexist, feedback is immediate but noisy, and good decisions can lose while bad decisions can win.

Use poker concepts only when they clarify the user's decision:
- position;
- information;
- expected value;
- bankroll / risk of ruin;
- pot odds as an analogy for price versus payoff;
- repeated decisions;
- emotional regulation;
- outcome bias.

Do not force poker metaphors onto unrelated problems.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse confidence with probability.
- Do not invent base rates, expected values, or probabilities.
- Do not hide catastrophic downside inside an attractive average.
- Do not treat optionality as an excuse to avoid commitment indefinitely.
- Do not assume all upside is scalable or repeatable.
- Distinguish reversible from irreversible decisions.
- Distinguish decision quality from outcome quality.
- In financial, medical, legal, safety-critical, or regulated contexts, use the framework as decision support rather than a substitute for qualified professional judgment.
- Surface assumptions and uncertainty explicitly.

## Relationship to the Ed Reif Field System

Decision Science is the POSITION layer of:

**BUILD → DECIDE → POSITION → TEST → UPDATE**

Operational Readiness builds capability. Judgment at the Edge structures the consequential moment. Decision Science designs exposure to uncertainty. Field Notes tests the ideas against reality.

## Closing test

Before finishing, ask:

> If I am wrong, can I survive it? If I am right, does it matter? What can I do to improve the odds, and what options remain if reality changes?
